Why we moved off WebSockets

For three years, our real-time order book ran on a single hand-tuned WebSocket server. It served forty thousand concurrent connections from a single 4 vCPU box, which is genuinely impressive for the engineering team that built it. So why are we ripping it out?

The short answer: operational cost. Every deploy required draining traffic to a sister instance and reconnecting clients with exponential backoff. Every TLS cert rotation triggered a thundering herd. Every connection-tracking firewall rule was a footgun. When a single engineer left who had owned that codebase, the operational risk became untenable.

What we moved to

Long-polling. Seriously. The latency penalty for our workload turned out to be in the single-digit milliseconds — far below what users can perceive in an order-book UI — and the operational surface area is now an HTTP load balancer we already had.

I don't want to oversell this. WebSockets are still the right answer for chat, multiplayer games, and anything that fans out server-side events to many clients at once. For our request/response-with-low-jitter workload, they were a luxury we didn't need.

Lessons

  • Operational cost compounds quietly. Measure it before it's a crisis.
  • Bus-factor risk is a real cost, even when nothing has gone wrong yet.
  • "Simpler" is sometimes faster too, once you account for retries.
